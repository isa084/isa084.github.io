## Introduction

Benchmark scores are often presented as headline numbers that look more self-contained than they really are. A single number is easy to communicate, but it does not necessarily convey the factors that produced it: the compiler version, target options, measurement method, clock policy, and thermal state. To examine this, I set out to reproduce Sipeed's published CoreMark result for the Lichee Pi 4A, a RISC-V single-board computer based on T-Head's TH1520.

Reproducing a published benchmark is a useful diagnostic step. Whether you are evaluating a board as a hobbyist or integrating a processor into a product, matching a known result helps establish that the hardware, software, toolchain, and measurement setup are working as expected before moving to more complex workloads. However, matching the number is not enough on its own: the conditions that produced it also need to be understood.

I treated the reproduction as a controlled system experiment: I started with the official source code, matched the vendor toolchain, verified the delivered clock, changed one factor at a time, and recorded the frequency and thermal state alongside the results. My best result reached 12,897 iterations per second, or **97.6% of Sipeed's figure**.

The results showed that:

- Compiler and target options improved performance by 1.77×.
- Operating-system controls such as CPU governor selection, core pinning, and clock pinning improved performance by only 0.1% on an idle board. They made the operating conditions explicit and repeatable, rather than making the benchmark faster.
- Cooling had no effect below the thermal limit of 85 °C. Once the uncooled CPU reached that limit and started throttling, performance fell by 15%.

The broader lesson is simple: **a benchmark score is a property of the system, not of the processor in isolation.**

## The board and benchmark

### Lichee Pi 4A: a RISC-V SBC built around T-Head's TH1520

The Lichee Pi 4A is a single-board computer from Sipeed built around the TH1520 system-on-chip (SoC). It has four RISC-V C910 cores running at up to 1.85 GHz. Each core has a 64 KB instruction cache and a 64 KB data cache, backed by 1 MB of shared L2 cache. My board has 8 GB of LPDDR4X memory and 32 GB of eMMC storage. These specifications come from [Sipeed's board documentation](https://wiki.sipeed.com/hardware/en/lichee/th1520/lpi4a/1_intro.html).

T-Head, Alibaba's semiconductor business, positions the C910 as a high-performance, out-of-order application processor for consumer and edge-computing systems.

### What CoreMark measures

[CoreMark](https://www.eembc.org/coremark/) is a small, portable CPU benchmark used to evaluate embedded processors. It repeatedly runs several integer workloads, including linked-list processing, matrix operations, a state machine, and a cyclic redundancy check. It reports the number of iterations completed per second. A higher score means that this particular workload completed faster. CoreMark is used on targets ranging from small microcontrollers to high-end 64-bit processors.

CoreMark is useful for quick comparisons and for studying the interaction between a CPU and its compiler. Its narrow scope is also its limitation: the workload is CPU-centric, with limited activity outside the CPU and its caches. It is therefore not a reliable proxy for memory bandwidth, I/O, floating-point, vector performance, or complete application behaviour.

Despite these limitations, CoreMark is widely reported by processor and board vendors. Sipeed publishes it for the C910, giving me a concrete result to reproduce.

## The target and method

The standard CoreMark result is reported in iterations per second. Since that score also depends on operating frequency, I use **CoreMark/MHz** alongside it to make results at different clock speeds easier to compare. This normalization is useful for this compute-bound workload, but should not be generalized to I/O- or memory-bound benchmarks.

The per-MHz score is derived as follows:

$$
\text{CoreMark/MHz} =
\frac{N_{\text{iterations}}}
     {t_{\text{seconds}} \times f_{\text{MHz}}}
$$

Here, $N_{\text{iterations}}$ is the number of iterations completed, $t_{\text{seconds}}$ is the elapsed wall-clock time, and $f_{\text{MHz}}$ is the operating frequency in MHz. Numerically, the result expresses the number of benchmark iterations completed per million clock cycles.

Using these two metrics, Sipeed's [benchmark page](https://wiki.sipeed.com/hardware/en/lichee/th1520/lpi4a/9_benchmark.html) provides two single-core runs at 1.85 GHz:

| Build | Compiler | Iterations/s | CoreMark/MHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public toolchain | GCC 13.1.0 | 9,175 | 4.96 |
| T-Head toolchain | GCC 10.4.0 (T-Head patched) | **13,220** | **7.15** |

For reproducibility, Sipeed's recipe uses the official [EEMBC CoreMark](https://github.com/eembc/coremark) source and the following compiler options:

```bash
make XCFLAGS="-march=rv64gv0p7_zfh_xtheadc -O3 -funroll-all-loops -finline-limit=500 \
  -fgcse-sm -fno-schedule-insns -msignedness-cmpiv -fno-code-hoisting \
  -mno-thread-jumps1 -mno-iv-adjust-addr-cost -mno-expand-split-imm"
```

### Test setup

Some conditions were held constant throughout the optimization ladder, while the governor, core affinity, and clock limits were deliberately introduced during its first three steps. The common setup was:

- **System:** Lichee Pi 4A with 8 GB RAM, running RevyOS 2026-05 (Linux 6.6.137, Debian 13).
- **Compiler:** XuanTie GCC V2.8.0, which is GCC 10.4.0 with T-Head's patches, used to cross-compile from x86-64. Sipeed used this version for their public-facing data, and I have used the same for consistency.
- **Cooling:** A heatsink, a thermal pad, and an OS-controlled fan were fitted on the board for most experiments, unless stated otherwise. The impact of these choices on temperature, CPU frequency, and benchmark performance is discussed later.
- **Optimization runs:** Three per build, using CoreMark's automatic iteration count so that each ran for at least 10 seconds. Variability was 0.1% to 1%, and I reported the median.

### Controlling the governor, core, and clock

A CPU-frequency governor determines how Linux selects an operating frequency in response to load. RevyOS uses the `performance` governor by default, which requests the highest permitted frequency. For the baseline run, I deliberately selected `schedutil`, which adjusts the requested frequency using the scheduler's estimate of CPU demand. Linux exposes these controls through tools such as `cpupower` and through its `cpufreq` interface in `sysfs`.

Linux may also move a process between cores or change its frequency while it is running. I did not apply the final controls at the outset because I wanted to measure their effects rather than assume that they mattered. Starting from the `schedutil` baseline with no core or clock pinning, I first restored the default `performance` governor, then bound CoreMark to core 3 using `taskset`, and finally set both the minimum and maximum CPU frequency to 1.848 GHz. Each control remained in place for the subsequent rows of the optimization ladder.

### Verifying the clock

After setting the requested clock, I checked the frequency that the CPU actually delivered. I wrote a small `perf_event_open` microbenchmark that estimated frequency using the clock cycle counter and elapsed wall-clock time. Requested frequencies of 1,500 MHz and 1,848 MHz measured **1,499.4 MHz** and **1,847.2 MHz** across repeated runs (about **0.04%** below nominal). Satisfied with the stability, I used the nominal 1,848 MHz for the experiments below.

## The optimization ladder

I started by compiling the benchmark with plain `-O2` and using the `schedutil` governor for frequency selection. I then changed one factor at a time: system controls first, compiler options next. All runs used the same board on the same day, with the heatsink properly mounted. Each row shows the cumulative configuration and reports both the raw and frequency-normalized CoreMark scores.

| # | Cumulative change | Iterations/s | CoreMark/MHz | Step | What it showed |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 0 | Baseline: `-O2`, `schedutil`, no pinning | 7,278 | 3.94 | – | Starting point |
| 1 | `performance` governor | 7,284 | 3.94 | +0.1% | RevyOS's default; no material gain |
| 2 | Pin the task to core 3 | 7,283 | 3.94 | 0% | Better control, not more speed |
| 3 | Pin the clock at 1.848 GHz | 7,289 | 3.94 | +0.1% | Better control, not more speed |
| 4 | `-O3` | 8,094 | 4.38 | **+11%** | First clear compiler gain |
| 5 | T-Head instructions (`-march=…_xtheadc`) | 10,314 | 5.58 | **+27%** | Largest single improvement |
| 6 | `-funroll-all-loops -finline-limit=500` | 11,710 | 6.34 | **+14%** | Second-largest improvement |
| 7 | `-fgcse-sm -fno-schedule-insns -fno-code-hoisting` | 12,120 | 6.56 | +3.5% | Modest but repeatable gain |
| 8 | T-Head-only options (`-msignedness-cmpiv` …) | 12,859 | 6.96 | **+6.1%** | Vendor-specific tuning still mattered |
| 9 | Sipeed's `-march=rv64gv0p7_zfh_xtheadc` | **12,897** | **6.98** | +0.3% | Final result: 97.6% of Sipeed's score |

The table separates into two clear groups. Rows 1–3 controlled how Linux scheduled and clocked the workload, but added almost no performance. Rows 4–9 changed the generated machine code and improved the score by 77%. For this benchmark, the compiler mattered much more than the operating-system controls.

Run-to-run spread was tiny: within ±0.1% for every row except #5, which varied by about 1%. In a separate test, `-mtune=c910` also made no difference: 6.96 CoreMark/MHz either way.

### A negative result: smaller instructions were not faster

Sipeed's `rv64gv0p7` target includes version 0.7.1 of the RISC-V vector extension, but the compiler emitted no vector instructions for CoreMark. Vector support therefore did not produce the row 9 result.

The other detail is what the target omits. RISC-V's compressed instruction extension (`c`) allows many common instructions to use smaller 16-bit encodings. The `rv64g` target does not include it, whereas `rv64gc` does. I expected the larger uncompressed build to lose performance through extra instruction-fetch and cache pressure. However, an independent test showed that the compressed and uncompressed builds differed by less than 0.1%. **For this CoreMark build on the C910, instruction fetch did not appear to be a bottleneck.**

## Thermal throttling and cooling

The optimization runs remained stable while the board was properly cooled. To isolate the effect of cooling, I kept the best configuration unchanged, using the same binary, core, and 1.848 GHz clock. I varied only the cooling arrangement.

| Cooling | Chip temperature | Clock actually delivered | Iterations/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heatsink mounted with a thermal pad, fan on | 53 → 55 °C | 1,848 MHz, all the time | 12,871–12,914 |
| Heatsink resting loosely on the chip, fan on | 53 → 61 °C | 1,848 MHz, all the time | 12,869–12,880 |
| Bare chip, starting warm | 66 → 85 °C, still climbing | 1,848 MHz, all the time | 12,870–12,888 |
| Bare chip, steady-state throttling (final 12 of 14 continuation runs) | held at 83–85 °C | 65% at 1,608 MHz, 34% at 1,500 MHz, 1% at 1,704 MHz | 10,906–11,054 (**−15%**) |
| Heatsink put back, starting hot | 61 → 65 °C | 1,848 MHz, all the time | 12,871–12,889 |

<img src="/assets/img/th1520/thermal-cooling-performance.png" alt="Temperature transients for three cooling arrangements and CoreMark performance before and after thermal throttling" style="display: block; width: 100%; height: auto;">

### Observations

- **Below 85 °C, temperature did not affect performance.** A mounted heatsink, a loose heatsink, and no heatsink all produced the same score while the CPU delivered 1.848 GHz.
- **At 85 °C, the kernel intervened.** Once the bare chip reached the thermal limit, the system's `cpufreq` cooling policy reduced the clock, mostly to 1,608 MHz, and maintained a temperature of 83–85 °C. As a result, the score fell by 15%. CoreMark still printed "Correct operation validated", but the temperature and frequency logs revealed the throttling.
- **Recovery was immediate.** Replacing the heatsink restored the full clock and a score of 12,886 on the first run.

Without the temperature and frequency logs, this 15% thermal loss could easily be mistaken for a software regression.

## What this result means

The number 12,897 is meaningful only with the conditions that produced it. The benchmark source, compiler and flags, operating frequency, OS configuration, cooling, and measurement method are part of the result, not optional details.

In this experiment, the compiler determined most of the performance. System-level controls made the operating state explicit and repeatable, while cooling preserved the full clock frequency once the processor approached its thermal limit. Reproducing the score required treating the compiler, operating system, clock delivery, and thermal management as parts of one measurement system.

## What next?

Possible extensions of this work:

- **Investigate the remaining 2.4%:** Sipeed's claimed score came from a dynamically linked build, whereas mine was statically linked and cross-compiled. The vendor kernel and firmware are other differences to isolate.
- **Extend the system characterization:** measure multicore scaling and power across the available frequency range, then apply workload-specific tests to memory, storage, and network performance.
- **Test how general the method is:** use broader application workloads and repeat the controlled comparison on other Arm and RISC-V boards.

If there is another benchmark or configuration that would make the comparison more useful, please suggest it. I will release the build and measurement scripts once the repository is tidied up.
